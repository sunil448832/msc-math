## Solutions
**Q1 — (B).** Differentiating $y=px+f(p)$ gives $p=p+(x+f'(p))\thinspace p'$, so $(x+f'(p))\thinspace p'=0$. The factor $p'=0$ gives the lines $y=cx+f(c)$. The factor $x+f'(p)=0$ gives the singular solution, which is the envelope of those lines. In (A), the condition should be $\partial/\partial c$ of the family, not $f(c)=0$.

**Q2 — (A).** From $x+2p=0$ we get $p=-x/2$, so $y=-\frac{x^2}{2}+\frac{x^2}{4}=-\frac{x^2}{4}$, i.e. $x^2+4y=0$. Option (B) has the sign error.

**Q3 — (D).** The lines $y=cx+a\sqrt{1+c^2}$ are at distance $a$ from the origin (all tangent to the circle of radius $a$). Their envelope is $x^2+y^2=a^2$.

**Q4 — (C).** The $p$-discriminant contains $E\thinspace T^2\thinspace C$, and the $c$-discriminant contains $E\thinspace N^2\thinspace C^3$. The tac-locus does **not** appear in the $c$-discriminant, so II is false.

**Q5 — (C).** The $c$-discriminant of $c^2+2xc+x^2-y=0$ is $4x^2-4(x^2-y)=4y$, so $y=0$. This curve also satisfies the ODE and is not of the form $(x+c)^2$. It is the envelope, so A is true. R is false: the $p$-discriminant can also contain tac-loci and cusp-loci, which are generally not solutions.

**Q6 — (C).** A tac-locus is the locus of points where two non-consecutive curves of the family touch. In general it does not satisfy the equation. Statements (A), (B) and (D) are standard facts.

**Q7 — (B).** (a) $x^2+4y=0$ (see Q2). (b) From $x-a/p^2=0$ we get $p=\sqrt{a/x}$, so $y=2\sqrt{ax}$ and $y^2=4ax$. (c) The envelope of the tangents $y=cx+\sqrt{a^2c^2+b^2}$ to the ellipse is the ellipse itself. (d) From $x=3p^2$ we get $y=p(x-p^2)=\frac{2px}{3}$, so $y^2=\frac{4x^2}{9}\cdot\frac{x}{3}=\frac{4x^3}{27}$.

**Q8 — (A).** Since $P=\frac{dY}{dX}=\frac{y}{x}p$, we have $p=\frac{xP}{y}$. Then $px-y=\frac{XP-Y}{y}$ and $py+x=x(P+1)$, so the equation becomes $(XP-Y)(P+1)=a^2P$. That is, $Y=PX-\frac{a^2P}{P+1}$, which is in Clairaut form. Its general solution is $y^2=cx^2-\frac{a^2c}{c+1}$.

**Q9 — (D).** Under these hypotheses, Picard's theorem gives uniqueness through every point of $D$. A singular solution would touch some member of the family at a point, giving two solutions through that point. So singular solutions can exist only where uniqueness fails.

**Q10 — (B).** Differentiating gives $x-c=-yp$, so $y^2p^2+y^2=1$. The envelope of the unit circles centred on the $x$-axis is $y=\pm1$, and these lines satisfy the ODE (with $p=0$). $y=0$ (option A) is the line of centres, which is not a solution.

**Q11 — (A).** $\dfrac{M_y-N_x}{N}=\dfrac{2y-y}{xy}=\dfrac1x$, which depends on $x$ only. So the integrating factor is $e^{\int dx/x}=x$. Check: $\partial_y\big(x^3+xy^2+x^2\big)=2xy=\partial_x(x^2y)$. Option (B) inverts it.

**Q12 — (D).** $y=0$ satisfies the ODE, and it is not $(x-c)^3$ for any $c$ (putting $c=0$ gives $y=x^3$, so (A) is wrong). The $c$-discriminant gives $3(x-c)^2=0$, so $x=c$ and $y=0$. Every cubic touches $y=0$ at its inflection point, so $y=0$ is the envelope and hence a singular solution. This is consistent with the non-uniqueness at $y=0$.

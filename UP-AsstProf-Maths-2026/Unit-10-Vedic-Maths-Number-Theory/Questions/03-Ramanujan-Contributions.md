## Solutions
**Q1 — (A).** Ramanujan was born on 22 December 1887. The day was declared National Mathematics Day in December 2011, and 2012, his 125th birth anniversary year, was National Mathematical Year. 26 April 1920 is the day he died, and 14 March is "Pi day".

**Q2 — (C).** The partitions of 7 are: 7, 6+1, 5+2, 5+1+1, 4+3, 4+2+1, 4+1+1+1, 3+3+1, 3+2+2, 3+2+1+1, 3+1⁴, 2+2+2+1, 2+2+1³, 2+1⁵, 1⁷. That is 15. (11 is $p(6)$.)

**Q3 — (D).** These are Ramanujan's congruences: $p(5n+4)\equiv0\ (5)$, $p(7n+5)\equiv0\ (7)$ and $p(11n+6)\equiv0\ (11)$. Also $\tau(n)\equiv\sigma_{11}(n)\pmod{691}$, where 691 comes from the numerator of $B_{12}$.

**Q4 — (B).** $1729 = 1^3+12^3 = 9^3+10^3$, the "taxicab" number. It also happens to be a Carmichael number ($7\cdot13\cdot19$), but it is **not** the smallest one, which is 561. So (C) is false as stated. (D) is false too: 7, 13, 19 are not consecutive primes.

**Q5 — (B).** $\tau$ is multiplicative and $\gcd(2,3)=1$, so $\tau(6) = (-24)(252) = -6048$. (D) adds instead of multiplying.

**Q6 — (B).** He was elected FRS (May 1918) and Fellow of Trinity (October 1918). III is false: he was born in **Erode** and grew up in Kumbakonam.

**Q7 — (A).** Multiplicativity of $\tau$ was conjectured by Ramanujan (1916) and proved by Mordell (1917). In modern language it follows because $\Delta$ spans the one-dimensional space $S_{12}(SL_2(\mathbb Z))$, so it is an eigenform of every Hecke operator $T_n$ with eigenvalue $\tau(n)$. R is the reason for A.

**Q8 — (D).** $d(72)=12 = d(60)$ and $60\lt 72$, so 72 does not beat every smaller number. The highly composite numbers begin 1, 2, 4, 6, 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, 120, 180, 240, 360, 720, 840, …

**Q9 — (D).** $\gcd(6,2) = 2$, so $c_6(2) = \mu(6)\cdot1 + \mu(3)\cdot2 = 1 - 2 = -1$. Useful special cases: $c_q(1)=\mu(q)$ and $c_q(0)=\varphi(q)$.

**Q10 — (C).** $R_2 = 11$. For $x\ge11$ there are always at least 2 primes in $(x/2, x]$. For $x=10$, the interval $(5,10]$ contains only the prime 7. The sequence is $2, 11, 17, 29, 41, \dots$

**Q11 — (A).** $`\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{q^{n^2}}{(q;q)_n}=\prod_{n\ge0}\frac{1}{(1-q^{5n+1})(1-q^{5n+4})}`$. The parts allowed on the right are $\equiv1,4\pmod5$. (B) is the second identity, which counts parts $\ge2$ differing by at least 2. (C) is Euler's odd parts = distinct parts.

**Q12 — (C).** $p(n)\sim\frac{1}{4n\sqrt3}\exp\left(\pi\sqrt{2n/3}\right)$ (Hardy–Ramanujan, 1918). Their circle method gives this as the leading term.

**Q13 — (C).** The Ramanujan–Nagell equation has solutions $n=3,4,5,7,15$ with $x=1,3,5,11,181$. Check: $2^{15}-7 = 32761 = 181^2$.
